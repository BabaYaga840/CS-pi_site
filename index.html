Abstract

Satisfy a deployment-time constraint without leaving the policy

A flow-matching policy deployed in the field must often satisfy a constraint that was absent from its demonstrations, such as a collision-free path or a particular grasp orientation. Existing training-free samplers take one of two approaches. Guidance steers the denoising trajectory with a constraint gradient at noisy intermediates: the gradient at a noisy sample need not point toward a clean feasible one. Projection maps the finished sample onto the feasible set and executes it: it is efficient, but the result is an action the policy would never draw.

We find that this dichotomy need not hold. Our key insight is that the projection is a cheap, clean, feasible signal, and the only mistake is executing it. We introduce Constraint-Satisfying Flow Policy Sampling (CS-Pi): we draw from the policy, project the clean sample to the nearest feasible point, and resample from the flow model biased toward that point. Iterating drives the samples toward constraint satisfaction with no noisy-state gradient and no auxiliary network; the method extends to arbitrary costs with a single tweak in the projection function. We evaluate on a 2D diagnostic, a 7-DoF shelf pick-and-place, and a zero-shot Franka deployment. Among constraint-satisfying samplers, CS-Pi stays closest to the policy, and it attains the highest safe-success on a real-world obstacle avoidance task.

The idea

Four ways to enforce a constraint, one that keeps the policy

The sampled action must be both safe, satisfying the constraint, and plausible, remaining likely under the policy. An action the policy would essentially never draw may be jerky, dynamically infeasible, or task-incoherent even when it is geometrically safe, since the policy distribution encodes smoothness, kinematic feasibility, and task structure that the constraint alone does not.

Gradient guidance perturbs each denoising step by the gradient of a constraint cost, and that reliance on a gradient is exactly what limits it. A hard constraint such as collision-freeness carries no usable gradient. Even for a smooth cost, the constraint lives on the clean action while guidance acts at noisy states, so it must approximate the gradient of the expected cost by the cost of the expected action, a proxy that is least accurate exactly when the policy is uncertain between modes.

Rejection and projection act only on the final action. Rejection keeps every output in-distribution, but the wait is intractable when feasible actions are rare; projection is efficient, but its output can be jerky or task-irrelevant. CS-Pi keeps projection's efficiency and rejection's plausibility: when a projected action is out-of-distribution, it does not restart from fresh noise, but resamples from a reduced noise level around the projection.

Method

Draw, project, renoise to n = k/(k+δ), re-run

Projection as a resampling target. The projection ac = 𝒫(â) of the current clean draw is cheap to compute, feasible by construction, and closer than â to the feasible actions the policy is likely to draw. It tells us where the next draw should concentrate, without executing it. The idealized target biases the policy toward ac while preserving its structure: q(a) ∝ πθ(a) · 𝒩(a; ac, σ²I).

The residual sets the width. We set σ = δ/k from the projection residual δ = ‖â − ac‖. A small δ means the previous draw was nearly feasible, so the policy almost certainly has in-distribution mass near ac and the next draw should concentrate there. A large δ means ac may sit where the policy assigns vanishing mass, so the next draw should be loose enough to reach a different policy mode (right of the obstacle rather than left). The coefficient k is the only hyperparameter, and it slides between the two classical extremes: k → 0 recovers rejection, k → ∞ recovers project-and-execute.

Partial renoising realises the target. Sampling q exactly is intractable, since the policy density is defined only through the ODE. Instead we renoise the projection to level n = k/(k+δ) and re-run the unmodified policy from there, feeding each new sample back until it lands in 𝒞.

Algorithm 1  Constraint-Satisfying Flow Policy Sampling
 1  a₀ ~ 𝒩(0, I);  â⁽⁰⁾ ← πθ(a₀; 0)                ▷ initial policy draw
 2  for i = 0, …, Kmax−1:
 3      ac⁽ⁱ⁾ ← 𝒫(â⁽ⁱ⁾);  δ⁽ⁱ⁾ ← ‖â⁽ⁱ⁾ − ac⁽ⁱ⁾‖
 4      if δ⁽ⁱ⁾ ≤ τ:  return â⁽ⁱ⁾
 5      n⁽ⁱ⁾ ← k / (k + δ⁽ⁱ⁾);  ε⁽ⁱ⁾ ~ 𝒩(0, I)
 6      an⁽ⁱ⁾ ← n⁽ⁱ⁾ ac⁽ⁱ⁾ + (1 − n⁽ⁱ⁾) ε⁽ⁱ⁾
 7      â⁽ⁱ⁺¹⁾ ← πθ(an⁽ⁱ⁾; n⁽ⁱ⁾)              ▷ re-denoise from level n
 8  return 𝒫(â⁽ᴷᵐᵃˣ⁾)                            ▷ optional terminal projection

Soft preferences as continuous costs. Deployment also brings preferences, behaviours to favour rather than boundaries to clear, such as keeping the gripper near-vertical while carrying a full cup. CS-Pi uses the projection only to produce a target for the next draw, so for a continuous cost L that target comes instead from the proximal operator proxβL(â) = argmina′ L(a′) + ‖a′ − â‖² / 2β; the rest of the algorithm is unchanged. Hard and soft requirements then compose by priority: applied in sequence, the operators pull toward the preferred orientation first, then project onto the collision-free set, where a gradient method would have to merge both into a single objective.

Results

CS-Pi departs from the policy only as far as the constraint forces

For each sampler we measure two things: whether it satisfies the constraint, and whether its samples stay likely under the policy. The primary metric is safe success, the fraction of rollouts that satisfy the constraint and complete the task. We compare against gradient guidance, Trust Sampling, rejection, projection, the unconstrained prior, and two control-barrier-function samplers (SafeDiffuser and SafeFlowMatcher), each swept over its main hyperparameter and reported at its best configuration; CS-Pi has a single hyperparameter, k. NFE counts flow-network forward passes and is not comparable across benchmarks. The CS-Pi row is highlighted; best per column is bold.

2D Avoidance: seeing the whole distribution

Demonstrations reach the goal along distinct paths, so the prior is genuinely multimodal, mirroring manipulation data where demonstrations of one task take different routes. Because the action space is two-dimensional, we can plot every sampler's entire output distribution. At inference we impose obstacles the prior never saw, in six scenarios of varying difficulty.

Composing a hard constraint with a soft preference

A feasibility set cannot express a preference among feasible actions. On the same 2D environment we add to the obstacle constraint a soft preference toward a height yref = 0.7. CS-Pi applies the two in sequence by priority, whereas the baselines must fold them into one weighted cost; only CS-Pi satisfies both.
