The projection is not the action

Constraint-satisfying sampling for pretrained flow policies

Enforce a constraint the demonstrations never had — without retraining, without a constraint gradient, and without executing anything the policy would not have drawn itself.

Soumojit Bhattacharya · CS-Pi · 20 min

~30 s · say the title as a claim, then move

The title is the argument, so read it out loud and let it sit for a beat. Everything in the next twenty minutes either sets it up or tests it.

One sentence of framing before you advance: "This is a sampling method. The policy is frozen, and we never take a gradient of the constraint." That rules out the two things people will otherwise assume you did.

Budget for the whole talk: ~3 min problem, ~4 min why the obvious fixes fail, ~6 min the method, ~5 min results, ~2 min wrap. If you are past the turn at 8 minutes you are on time.

I · The problemThe bind

A deployed policy has to satisfy two things at once

1 · Satisfy the constraint

  • Obstacles, joint limits, workspace bounds, a required end-effector pose — none of it was in the demonstrations, and it changes between deployments.
  • Retraining per constraint is exactly what we are trying to avoid.

2 · Stay in the policy's distribution

  • Not an aesthetic preference — a safety property.
  • Off-distribution actions are where the learned dynamics stop being predictive, where trajectories stop being smooth, and where the arm does things nobody trained it to do.
Today you pick one. The strongest constraint-satisfying baseline on our shelf task is feasible 96% of the time and pays for it with samples the policy assigns almost no mass to.
And the strongest in-distribution method is the unmodified policy, which collides in 82% of the same tasks.
The claim of this talk: you do not have to pick.
~1.5 min · the frame for everything after it

Requirement 2 is the one that needs defending, so spend your words there. An audience accepts "don't hit things" instantly and hears "stay in-distribution" as a modelling nicety. It isn't.

"In-distribution is a safety property. Off the data manifold, nothing you validated about the policy still holds."

The two callouts on the right are the bind in measured numbers, and they set up the whole middle of the talk: every existing method sits at one end or the other.

Land the last line as a promise, then go straight to the pipeline — do not start listing baselines yet.

II · Two attemptsAttempt 1 · gradient guidance

Bend the velocity field toward feasibility

draw−α ∇C at every step→execute

The gradient is taken through a proxy: a one-step guess at the final action. Early in the flow that guess is a blur across both routes, not a plan.
Pushing a blur toward feasibility lands between the routes: feasible, and nothing the policy ever does. So: leave the velocities alone and start from a real draw.
~1.5 min · the weakness here is what motivates everything after it

Expect sympathy for guidance in the room, so be precise rather than dismissive. The picture does the work: the red dot is out of the obstacle, and it is in the one place the policy never goes.

Why it lands there: the gradient needs a clean action to evaluate the constraint on, and mid-flow there isn't one. Guidance substitutes the posterior-mean guess, which while the policy is still undecided between the two routes sits between them. Nudging that guess toward feasibility nudges a path no route of the policy proposes.

Second, briefly: a hard constraint has no usable gradient. Distance to a box is flat away from the boundary and non-smooth at it, so guidance enforces a smoothed surrogate instead.

"Altering velocities with a gradient of a proxy is how you leave the data. So whatever we do, we do it to a finished draw, not to the flow."

"Just tune α?" — at the weight where it stops colliding it also stops doing the task: on the shelf, tuned guidance reaches 0.0% collisions and 28% goal reach against the prior's 72%. There is no good setting, only a trade.

II · Two attemptsAttempt 2 · projection

Draw first, then snap the answer onto the feasible set

draw â→project onto C → c, δ→execute c

Feasible by construction, no gradient, closed form for boxes, spheres, half-spaces and joint limits. So: we keep the projection.
But c is the nearest feasible point, not the most likely one. It sits off the policy's mass, and executing it means executing an action the policy never learned.
~1.5 min · be generous — the turn has nowhere to come from otherwise

Do not trash projection. It is the strongest baseline in the paper, and the entire idea of the method is that it is almost right. If you dismiss it here, the next slide lands as a trick instead of an insight.

Walk the arrow: the draw is inside the obstacle, so we move it the shortest distance that makes it feasible. That distance is δ, and the point we reach is c. Both are free.

Then say the defect in exactly these words, because the phrasing recurs at the turn: c is chosen for proximity, not for likelihood. Look where it sits: on the rim of the obstacle, outside the grey. Nearest is not most-likely, and in a bimodal policy the nearest feasible point is often on the wrong side of the obstacle entirely.

"Projection gets a feasible answer. It just gets it by leaving the model."

~1.5 min · the hinge of the talk — slow down and let the fork land

Pause before you advance to this slide. Then lay out the fork plainly: we are holding a feasible point c that we know is off-distribution. There are exactly three things you can do with it.

One: execute it. That is projection, and we just saw why not.

Two: throw it away and draw again from scratch. That is rejection sampling, and the picture shows the cost: every redraw is independent, so most of them land right back in the obstacle, and nothing we learned from the failure — not c, not δ — is used. It is statistically correct and it costs 30× the compute on the shelf, degrading exactly when the constraint gets tight.

"The projection is a cheap, clean, gradient-free feasibility signal. It tells us where a feasible answer is and how far away we were. The mistake was executing it — the fix is to draw again, and let it tell the draw where to look."

Three is the method. Do not explain how yet; the next slide is the how.

III · The ideaCS-Pi · one pass

Redraw the policy through a window centred at c

draw â→project → c, δ→renoise c to n = k/(k+δ), re-run the flow→a′

a′  ∼  (1/Z) · pdata(a′) · exp(−k²‖a′ − c‖² / 2δ²)

the policy's own densityEvery redraw is still a draw from the prior. It cannot land off the grey, whatever the window does.
a Gaussian centred on the projected pointWidth δ/k. It pulls the redraw toward c, and so toward the feasible set, without ever leaving the prior.

In practice: partially renoise c to depth n = k/(k+δ), re-run the flow from there. One extra forward pass, no constraint gradient. That renoise-and-re-run is the product above.

~2 min · the method, in one picture and one line — do not rush the equation

Walk the picture first, then the equation. Picture: we put a window around c, sized by how badly we missed, and we draw the policy again through that window. The draw can only land where the window and the grey overlap, so it is on the data by construction, and it is pulled toward c, so it is closer to the feasible set than the draw we started from. Not there yet — closer.

Equation, two factors, and say them in this order. The first factor is the policy's own density: this is a real policy sample, not a bent one and not a snapped one. The second is a Gaussian at c with width δ/k: this is the bias. Multiply, normalise, draw.

How you get it for free: partially renoise c along the flow's interpolant to depth n = k/(k+δ) and re-run the flow. One Bayes step on the interpolant law says that re-running from there samples exactly this product; the identity is (1−n)/n = δ/k. Strictly the window sits at ĉ = c plus the injected noise scaled by δ/k, which is why it is a window and not a point.

"We're not steering the policy and we're not overwriting it. We're multiplying it by a Gaussian centred where the projection said feasible is — and the violation sets how wide that Gaussian is."

"Isn't that just guidance again?" — no gradient anywhere, and the prior is a factor rather than a starting point that gets pushed off. The 2D slide shows the difference: no ring.

Repeat. Every pass lands closer, and every pass is the policy's.

Because the window is centred on the feasible set and every redraw is in-distribution, δ shrinks pass over pass (δ₁ → δ₂ → 0 in the picture). Stop when δ is below tolerance, or cap the passes and project the last chunk.

~1 min · trace the chain once, then explain k

Trace the chain with your finger: draw, project, redraw through the window; project, redraw through a smaller window; feasible. Two things to point at: every blue dot is inside the grey, and the windows shrink, because each one is sized by the δ that came before it.

k is the only knob, and it sets the window width. The width is δ/k, so k decides how tightly the redraw hugs c. Small k, wide window: the redraw can wander far enough to switch routes, which is what a large δ needs, but the pull toward c is weak, so it takes more passes to converge. Large k, narrow window: the redraw stays close to c, δ collapses fast, but it can only refine the route it is already on. Equivalently, in the implementation: n = k/(k+δ), so a larger k renoises shallower for the same δ.

Then the honest headline: k = 20, fixed across all four benchmarks. Using per-benchmark values buys at most 4.3 points.

"Refine when you're close, reconsider when you're not — the same operation at two widths, and the width is set by the violation itself."

"Does it terminate?" — δ shrinks across passes in practice, and we cap the passes. If the cap is hit we project the final chunk to guarantee feasibility; that fires on 15.7% of shelf chunks and 4.8% on hardware.

V · WrapSay these before the audience does

Three things it doesn't do

  • It cannot invent behaviour the policy doesn't have. Every sample comes from the prior, so if no demonstrated behaviour clears the obstacle, no amount of renoising finds one. Success drops 1.00 → 0.91 when we add objects outside the demonstrated support.
  • It needs a projection operator. Closed form for boxes, spheres, half-spaces, joint limits, SO(3) targets. A general non-convex set is its own optimization problem.
  • Feasibility is asymptotic, not guaranteed. We iterate until δ is small, not until it is zero. A terminal projection restores the hard guarantee at the cost of one off-distribution chunk — it fires on 15.7% of shelf chunks and 4.8% on hardware.
The honest summary: we inherit the policy's coverage. That's the price of never retraining, and it is the thing I'd attack first.
~1 min · brisk, unapologetic, no hedging

Deliver these fast and flatly. The goal is not to soften them — it's to show you know exactly where the method ends, which is what makes the earlier claims credible.

The first one is the real limitation and the most interesting: the method's contribution is finding a feasible mode the policy already has. That predicts a dependence on demonstration coverage, and the 1.00 → 0.91 drop is the first evidence of it. The clean experiment — sweep the demonstration support and watch success track it — is the one I'd run next, and it's a good thing to say you'd run next.

If time is short, cut the second bullet and keep the first and third.

V · WrapTakeaway

The projection is a cheap, clean, gradient-free feasibility signal. The only mistake was ever executing it.

Use it to decide where to start sampling again — draw, project, renoise to n = k/(k+δ), re-run. No retraining, no constraint gradient, about one extra forward pass, and what you execute is always a sample the policy could have produced.

~1 min · land it and stop

Say the quote, then stop talking. Do not summarise the results again — the picture from the 2D slide is what they'll remember, and adding numbers now dilutes it.

If you want a closing line to invite questions, use the one that opens the most doors: "The part I'd most like to argue about is whether the prior's coverage is the real ceiling here."

Questions you should expect, in likelihood order: why not rejection sampling (30× compute, degrades as the constraint tightens); why not just tune guidance (no good setting — 0% collisions at 28% goal reach); how do you pick k (fixed at 20 everywhere, ≤4.3 points versus per-task tuning); does it guarantee feasibility (asymptotically, or exactly with the terminal projection); what if the constraint is infeasible (it degrades gracefully rather than diverging — there's a backup slide in the long deck).
