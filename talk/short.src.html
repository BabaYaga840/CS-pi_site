The projection is not the action

Constraint-satisfying sampling for pretrained flow policies

Enforce a constraint the demonstrations never had — without retraining, without a constraint gradient, and without executing anything the policy would not have drawn itself.

Soumojit Bhattacharya · CS-Pi · 20 min

~30 s · say the title as a claim, then move

The title is the argument, so read it out loud and let it sit for a beat. Everything in the next twenty minutes either sets it up or tests it.

One sentence of framing before you advance: "This is a sampling method. The policy is frozen, and we never take a gradient of the constraint." That rules out the two things people will otherwise assume you did.

Budget for the whole talk: ~3 min problem, ~4 min why the obvious fixes fail, ~6 min the method, ~5 min results, ~2 min wrap. If you are past the turn at 8 minutes you are on time.

I · The problemThe bind

A deployed policy has to satisfy two things at once

1 · Satisfy the constraint

  • Obstacles, joint limits, workspace bounds, a required end-effector pose — none of it was in the demonstrations, and it changes between deployments.
  • Retraining per constraint is exactly what we are trying to avoid.

2 · Stay in the policy's distribution

  • Not an aesthetic preference — a safety property.
  • Off-distribution actions are where the learned dynamics stop being predictive, where trajectories stop being smooth, and where the arm does things nobody trained it to do.
Today you pick one. The strongest constraint-satisfying baseline on our shelf task is feasible 96% of the time and pays for it with samples the policy assigns almost no mass to.
And the strongest in-distribution method is the unmodified policy, which collides in 82% of the same tasks.
The claim of this talk: you do not have to pick.
~1.5 min · the frame for everything after it

Requirement 2 is the one that needs defending, so spend your words there. An audience accepts "don't hit things" instantly and hears "stay in-distribution" as a modelling nicety. It isn't.

"In-distribution is a safety property. Off the data manifold, nothing you validated about the policy still holds."

The two callouts on the right are the bind in measured numbers, and they set up the whole middle of the talk: every existing method sits at one end or the other.

Land the last line as a promise, then go straight to the pipeline — do not start listing baselines yet.

II · Two attemptsAttempt 1 · gradient guidance

Push every step toward feasibility

The gradient is evaluated at a one-step estimate of the final action — early on, that estimate is an average over modes. A blur, not a plan.
Nudging a blur toward feasibility interpolates between routes. The interpolation of going left and going right around an obstacle goes straight through it.
~1.5 min · the mode-interpolation argument is the one to lead with

Expect sympathy for guidance in the room — classifier-free guidance is a well-loved tool. Be precise rather than dismissive.

Lead with mode interpolation because it is geometric and undeniable: if the policy is still undecided between left and right around the obstacle, the posterior-mean estimate sits between them — which is exactly where the obstacle is. The gradient there pushes on a path no mode of the policy actually proposes.

Second, briefly: a hard constraint gives no usable gradient. Distance to a box is flat away from the boundary and non-smooth at it, so guidance methods substitute a smoothed surrogate and then enforce the surrogate.

"And notice the shape of the failure: the flow never runs unmodified, so what comes out the end is not a sample from the policy at all."

"Just tune α?" — the honest answer, and worth 15 seconds: at the weight where it stops colliding it also stops doing the task. On the shelf, tuned guidance reaches 0.0% collisions and 28% goal reach, against the prior's 72%. There is no good setting, only a trade.

II · Two attemptsAttempt 2 · projection

Leave the flow alone and fix the answer instead

Everything about this is right: feasible by construction, no extra flow calls, no gradient needed, closed form for boxes, spheres, half-spaces, joint limits.
One thing is wrong: the snapped point is chosen for proximity, not likelihood — and then we execute it.
~1.5 min · be generous — the turn has nowhere to come from otherwise

Do not trash projection. It is the strongest baseline in the paper, and the entire idea of the method is that it is almost right. If you dismiss it here, the next slide lands as a trick instead of an insight.

Say the defect in exactly these words, because the phrasing recurs at the turn: the projection is chosen for proximity, not for likelihood. Nearest is not most-likely, and in a bimodal policy the nearest feasible point is often on the wrong side of the obstacle entirely.

One sentence for rejection sampling so nobody thinks you forgot it: "You could also redraw until something is feasible — that's statistically correct and it costs 30× more compute, and it degrades exactly when the constraint gets tight." Then move on.

"Projection gets a feasible answer. It just gets it by leaving the model."

III · The ideaThe turn

The projection was never the problem

The projection gives us a feasible target c and a violation magnitude δ, both for free, both without a gradient. Those are exactly the two things we need. The only mistake was ever running it.

~1 min · the hinge of the talk — slow down and let the cross land

Pause before you advance to this slide. Then say it plainly:

"The projection is a cheap, clean, gradient-free feasibility signal. The only mistake anyone made was executing it."

Point at the cut arrow. We keep the whole left-hand side of the pipeline and delete one arrow. That is the entire contribution, stated honestly, and stating it that modestly is what makes it convincing.

Then set up the obvious question so the next slide answers it rather than introducing itself: "So if we're not executing it — what is it for?"

~2 min · the method, in one diagram — do not rush the return arrow

Trace the loop with your finger, once, slowly: draw → project → renoise to n → re-run. Then say what makes it different from rejection sampling, because that is the question in the room:

"Rejection throws the sample away and restarts from pure noise. We restart from partway down the flow — and the projection tells us exactly how far."

The feedback law is the idea. δ is a free by-product of a computation we were doing anyway, and it is precisely the right signal: a sample that barely violated the constraint needs a small correction, so we renoise shallowly and refine it. A sample that was badly wrong needs a different plan, so we renoise deeply and the policy can commit to a different mode entirely.

Then the honest headline: k = 20, fixed across all four benchmarks. There is no per-task tuning here — using per-benchmark values buys at most 4.3 points.

"Does it terminate?" — δ shrinks across iterations in practice, and we cap the iterations. If the cap is hit we can project the final chunk to guarantee feasibility; that fires on 15.7% of shelf chunks and 4.8% on hardware.

Narrate one cycle, reading the numbers off the bar as they change:

  • The flow draws a sample — it goes straight through the obstacle.
  • δ is the violation. It's large.
  • Project to the nearest feasible point c. We do not execute it.
  • n is small, so we renoise deeply.
  • Re-run. New sample, smaller δ, larger n — now we only refine.

"Three iterations. And the thing we execute at the end is an ordinary draw from the policy — it just started from a better place."

III · The ideaWhy it stays in-distribution

Each iteration samples the prior through a window at the target

a′ ~ (1/Z) · pdata(a′) · e−k²‖a′−ĉ‖²/2δ²

  • Renoising the anchor to depth n and re-denoising draws from the policy prior multiplied by a Gaussian centred at the feasible target, with width σw = δ/k.
  • So we never leave the prior — pdata is a factor in every draw. We only reweight it toward feasibility.
  • And the width is the feedback law again: large δ opens the window wide enough to reach a different mode; small δ narrows it to a local refinement.
This is why constraint satisfaction doesn't cost likelihood here. On the shelf our samples score logL 18.0 against the unmodified prior's own 15.0.
~1.5 min · one slide of theory, and it earns its place

This is the only formal claim in the talk, so state what it buys and move: the method is not a heuristic that happens to stay near the data — the prior is literally a factor in the density it samples from.

Walk the two panels: same prior, same feasible set, two window widths. Wide window (large δ) — the blocked mode is suppressed and mass moves to the surviving mode, so the sample switches modes. Narrow window (small δ) — it just refines where it already was.

"One line of algebra, and it says the two behaviours we wanted — refine when you're close, reconsider when you're not — are the same operation at two widths."

Then the number that makes it concrete: logL 18.0 against the prior's 15.0. Our samples are more likely under the policy than the policy's own draws, because constraint satisfaction correlates with the clean demonstrated behaviour.

Proof is one Bayes step from the interpolant law — offer it for the Q&A rather than doing it live.

This slide is about generality and it makes its case visually — tell them to watch how the arms move, not whether they succeed.

The only change from the shelf is the projection operator: swap the feasible-set snap for an end-effector proximity step. Same loop, same code, same k.

The inversion is the interesting part. On the shelf, a collision is terminal, so trading likelihood for feasibility is obviously right. Here, missing the orientation slightly costs almost nothing — but leaving the manifold costs everything, because the arm executes the trajectory either way.

"Guidance satisfies the orientation with metre-scale jumps. That's not a trajectory, it's a teleport. We satisfy it and move like the original policy."

And say it's the friendliest possible setting for guidance — a dense, smooth, differentiable cost — so nobody thinks the task was chosen to break it.

V · WrapSay these before the audience does

Three things it doesn't do

  • It cannot invent behaviour the policy doesn't have. Every sample comes from the prior, so if no demonstrated behaviour clears the obstacle, no amount of renoising finds one. Success drops 1.00 → 0.91 when we add objects outside the demonstrated support.
  • It needs a projection operator. Closed form for boxes, spheres, half-spaces, joint limits, SO(3) targets. A general non-convex set is its own optimization problem.
  • Feasibility is asymptotic, not guaranteed. We iterate until δ is small, not until it is zero. A terminal projection restores the hard guarantee at the cost of one off-distribution chunk — it fires on 15.7% of shelf chunks and 4.8% on hardware.
The honest summary: we inherit the policy's coverage. That's the price of never retraining, and it is the thing I'd attack first.
~1 min · brisk, unapologetic, no hedging

Deliver these fast and flatly. The goal is not to soften them — it's to show you know exactly where the method ends, which is what makes the earlier claims credible.

The first one is the real limitation and the most interesting: the method's contribution is finding a feasible mode the policy already has. That predicts a dependence on demonstration coverage, and the 1.00 → 0.91 drop is the first evidence of it. The clean experiment — sweep the demonstration support and watch success track it — is the one I'd run next, and it's a good thing to say you'd run next.

If time is short, cut the second bullet and keep the first and third.

V · WrapTakeaway

The projection is a cheap, clean, gradient-free feasibility signal. The only mistake was ever executing it.

Use it to decide where to start sampling again — draw, project, renoise to n = k/(k+δ), re-run. No retraining, no constraint gradient, about one extra forward pass, and what you execute is always a sample the policy could have produced.

~1 min · land it and stop

Say the quote, then stop talking. Do not summarise the results again — the picture from the 2D slide is what they'll remember, and adding numbers now dilutes it.

If you want a closing line to invite questions, use the one that opens the most doors: "The part I'd most like to argue about is whether the prior's coverage is the real ceiling here."

Questions you should expect, in likelihood order: why not rejection sampling (30× compute, degrades as the constraint tightens); why not just tune guidance (no good setting — 0% collisions at 28% goal reach); how do you pick k (fixed at 20 everywhere, ≤4.3 points versus per-task tuning); does it guarantee feasibility (asymptotically, or exactly with the terminal projection); what if the constraint is infeasible (it degrades gracefully rather than diverging — there's a backup slide in the long deck).
